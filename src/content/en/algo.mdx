# Algorithm

This is a **constrained integer decomposition** problem: **given any total, find combinations that split it into several items (such as a bento or a drink) and a one-dollar item (such as a plastic bag)**, with these constraints:

- Within a single order, each item has exactly one price.
- The one-dollar item is optional.

## Items and Price Constraints

Suppose there are $n$ items available for making up the total. Item $i$ (for $i = 1, 2, \dots, n$) has its own set of valid prices $P_i \subseteq \mathbb{Z}^+$ (positive integers). From this set we pick a price $p_i \in P_i$ that satisfies a given last-digit rule, together with a quantity $q_i \in \mathbb{N}_0$ (each item may be used or skipped). For example:

- Bento (item 1, $i = 1$, price $p_1$, quantity $q_1$): price range $P_1$ between 90 and 150, with $p_1$ ending in 0, 5, or 9, for example 99, 120, 135.
- Drink (item 2, $i = 2$, price $p_2$, quantity $q_2$): price range $P_2$ between 40 and 90, with $p_2$ ending in 0 or 5, for example 45, 60, 85.

There is also a one-dollar item (such as a plastic bag, price $p_* = 1$, quantity $q_*$): each costs 1, with quantity 0 to 9, intended to make up the remaining change:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_1 &\in P_1 = \{\, p \in \mathbb{Z} : 90 \le p \le 150,\; p \bmod 10 \in \{0,5,9\} \,\} \\
p_2 &\in P_2 = \{\, p \in \mathbb{Z} : 40 \le p \le 90,\; p \bmod 10 \in \{0,5\} \,\} \\
&q_1, q_2 \in \mathbb{N}_0 \\
p_* &= 1, \quad q_* \in \{0, 1, \dots, 9\} \\
\end{aligned}
$$

- The same generalises to items 3, 4, and so on.
- Including the one-dollar item, there are at most 7 items in total ($n \le 6$ regular items plus the one-dollar item). In principle it could extend to more, but too many items are meaningless for this constrained decomposition problem.

## Problem Definition

Let the target total be $T \in \mathbb{Z}^+$. For each item $i \in \{1, 2, \dots, n\}$ we seek a price $p_i$ and quantity $q_i$, plus the quantity $q_*$ of the one-dollar item (unit price $p_* = 1$), such that:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(p_i \cdot q_i\big) + q_* = T
$$

If we want to make up the total with two items — a bento and a drink ($n = 2$) — plus the plastic bag, this becomes:

$$
\underbrace{\big(p_1 \cdot q_1\big)}_{\text{bento}} + \underbrace{\big(p_2 \cdot q_2\big)}_{\text{drink}} + \underbrace{q_*}_{\text{bag}} = T
$$

Let $\mathcal{F}$ be the feasible set of all solutions satisfying the equation and constraints above. We sort $\mathcal{F}$ by the user’s chosen strategy and return the best result:

- **Fewest bags first**: assumes that plastic bags at a bento shop are free and one bag can hold several bentos and drinks. We first minimise the bag count, then balance the quantities across items.

$$
\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\mathcal{F}}\; q_*
$$

- **Balanced quantities first**: assumes that someone buying a bento will also grab a drink, so quantities should be roughly equal. We first balance item quantities, then minimise the bag count.

$$
\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\mathcal{F}}\bigl(\max_i q_i - \min_i q_i\bigr)
$$

## Constraints

1. Within a single order, each item $i$ uses a single price $p_i$. If a pork-chop bento and a chicken-leg bento have different prices, they must be treated as separate items.
2. The chosen strategy decides the primary preference: “fewest bags first” minimises the bag count $q_*$; “balanced quantities first” makes the quantities $q_i$ as even as possible (avoiding combinations like 20 bentos with 4 drinks).
3. The plastic bag is optional ($q_* \ge 0$).
4. Each item may be used or skipped ($q_i \ge 0$), but they cannot all be skipped — the order cannot consist of plastic bags alone ($\sum_i q_i \ge 1$).
5. For a reasonable solution, the bag count should not exceed the total number of items ($q_* \le \sum_i q_i$). After all, nobody buys 2 bentos and then 10 extra plastic bags.
6. After obtaining a combination, verify that the total equals $T$.

## Why a One-Dollar Item?

The target total $T$ can be any positive integer, but each item’s price $p_i$ may be subject to a last-digit rule (for example, bentos end in 0, 5, 9 and drinks end in 0, 5). This means the amounts $\sum_i p_i q_i$ reachable with these items alone are discrete and sparse — they may not hit $T$ exactly.

Without the one-dollar item, the problem becomes “hit $T$ exactly under restricted prices”, which for many values of $T$ has no solution at all. For example, if bentos must end in 0 or 5 and drinks always end in 0, then every combination is a multiple of 5, and a total like 114 can never be reached.

The one-dollar item (like a plastic bag or disposable utensils) fills that gap: first approximate the total $T$ with a few items, then top up the remaining change with plastic bags.

## Solving Strategy

The approach has three steps: try every possible way to buy, filter out the unreasonable ones, then sort by preference and pick the best few.

Picture a multi-level decision tree: the first level decides “buy a bento or not? at which price? how many?”, the second level decides the drink, and so on. Expanding every choice at each level produces a branching tree. Each path from top to bottom corresponds to one complete purchase; at the end we check whether the remainder is reasonable.

We traverse this tree with depth-first search (DFS): follow one path all the way down (all items decided), record the result, then backtrack to the nearest branch and try the next choice, until every path has been explored.

For $T = 253$, with the bento at 100 and the drink at 75, the tree looks roughly like this:

```
T = 253
├─ bento ×0 ┬ drink ×0 → left 253 → bag 253  (✗ exceeds bag limit)
│           ├ drink ×1 → left 178 → bag 178  (✗ exceeds bag limit)
│           ├ drink ×2 → left 103 → bag 103  (✗ exceeds bag limit)
│           └ drink ×3 → left  28 → bag  28  (✗ exceeds bag limit)
├─ bento ×1 ┬ drink ×0 → left 153 → bag 153  (✗ exceeds bag limit)
│           ├ drink ×1 → left  78 → bag  78  (✗ exceeds bag limit)
│           └ drink ×2 → left   3 → bag   3  (✓ a feasible solution)
└─ bento ×2 ┬ drink ×0 → left  53 → bag  53  (✗ exceeds bag limit)
            └ ...
```

## A Worked Example

Suppose the target total is $T = 253$, with the bento priced at 100 and the drink at 75. DFS tries every quantity of bentos and drinks. For example:

- 2 bentos, 0 drinks: $100 \times 2 = 200$, leaving 53 — would need 53 bags, over the limit of 9. Rejected.
- 1 bento, 1 drink: $100 + 75 = 175$, leaving 78 — would need 78 bags. Rejected.
- 1 bento, 2 drinks: $100 + 75 \times 2 = 250$, leaving 3 — take 3 bags, exactly fills the gap. **Passes** and becomes a candidate.

Substituting the last set back into the equation to verify:

$$
\underbrace{\$100 \cdot 1}_{\text{bento}} + \underbrace{\$75 \cdot 2}_{\text{drink}} + \underbrace{\$1\cdot 3}_{\text{bag}} = \$253
$$

Although 253 cannot be made from bentos and drinks alone, topping up the 3 in change with plastic bags makes it work. Trying every price-and-quantity combination this way collects all the candidates.

Once all candidates are collected, they are sorted by the chosen strategy (“fewest bags first” or “balanced quantities first”). The top three plans with distinct price combinations are shown. Pressing “New set” excludes the plans already shown and lists the next batch.

## Pseudocode

```pseudocode
function decompose(T, items):          # items[1..n], each with a valid price set P_i
  candidates ← []

  function dfs(i, remaining):           # decide price and quantity of item i
    if i > n:                           # all items decided
      q_* ← remaining                   # remaining goes to plastic bags
      if q_* < 0 or q_* > 9: return
      if sum(q_i) = 0: return           # order cannot be bags only
      if q_* > sum(q_i): return         # bags must not outnumber items
      balance ← max(q_i) - min(q_i)     # smaller means more balanced
      candidates.push({ p_i, q_i, q_*, balance })
      return

    q_i ← 0                             # skip this item
    dfs(i + 1, remaining)

    for p_i in P_i:                     # pick a price × positive quantity
      for q_i in 1..floor(remaining / p_i):
        dfs(i + 1, remaining - p_i * q_i)

  dfs(1, T)

  # sort by the chosen strategy; take the first 3 with distinct prices:
  # mode = "bags"    → sort by (q_* asc, balance asc)   fewest bags first
  # mode = "balance" → sort by (balance asc, q_* asc)   balanced quantities first
  sort candidates by mode
  picks ← first 3 with distinct prices, relax if fewer

  # verify: each pick must satisfy Σ p_i * q_i + q_* = T
  return picks
```

## Time and Space Complexity

The complexity of this algorithm is the number of nodes visited in the decision tree. The key is the average branching factor at each level; multiplying the branch counts across all levels gives the total number of nodes visited.

### Step 1: branches per level

Let the target total be $T$. When DFS descends to level $i$, levels $1 \dots i-1$ have already spent some money. Let $R_i$ be the amount still needed to reach $T$ on entering level $i$:

$$
R_i = T - \sum_{k=1}^{i-1} p_k q_k \leq T
$$

The price set $P_i$ of item $i$ has cardinality $|P_i|$. At each visit to level $i$, the choices are:

- Skip this item ($q_i = 0$): just 1 possibility.
- For each price $p_i \in P_i$ ($|P_i|$ of them), try quantities $q_i$ from 1 up to $\lfloor R_i / p_i \rfloor$. Since $R_i \le T$ and $p_i \ge 1$, the quantity count is at most $T / p_i$, an upper bound of $O(T)$.

Combining the two, the branch count at level $i$ is about $1 + |P_i| \cdot O(T) = O(|P_i| \cdot T)$.

### Step 2: multiply across $n$ levels

Since DFS makes a choice at each level on the way down, the total node count is the product of the per-level branch counts:

$$
O\!\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n} |P_i| \cdot T\right)
$$

Once the price ranges and last-digit rules are set, they do not change. For example, if a bento’s price is restricted to 90–110 with last digits 0, 5, 9, then $P_1$ is just the six values $\{90, 95, 99, 100, 105, 109\}$. In other words, every $|P_i|$ is a constant that does not grow with $T$ and can be treated as a constant $K$. The only thing that grows with the input is the $T$ at each level:

$$
O\!\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n} |P_i| \cdot T\right) = O\big(|P_1|\cdot T\cdot|P_2|\cdot T\cdots\big) =O(\underbrace{|P_1|\cdot|P_2|}_{K}\cdots T^n) =O\!\left(T^{n}\right)
$$

Note that $n$ is in the **exponent** and $T$ is the **base** (not $O(n^{T})$): each extra item adds a DFS level and raises the order by one. For example, two items plus the plastic bag gives $n = 2$ and $O(T^2)$; at the limit $n = 6$ it grows to $O(T^{6})$. This is exactly why the item count $n$ is capped at a small constant.

### Step 3: candidates are one order fewer than nodes visited

The above counts the **nodes visited**, $O(T^n)$. But a path reaching the bottom is not necessarily feasible; it must also pass the constraint that the remainder lies in $0 \le q_* \le 9$.

From another angle: the plastic bag fills whatever is left after all $n$ items are decided. This constraint requires the subtotal from the first $n$ items, $\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i q_i$, to land in the interval $[\,T-9,\; T\,]$. Even though the first $n - 1$ items can be chosen freely ($O(T^{n-1})$ combinations), the last item is almost locked in by the earlier choices — it must squeeze the total into that narrow interval, leaving only $O(1)$ degrees of freedom. Multiplying the two, the number of actual candidates is $O(T^{n-1})$, one order fewer than the nodes visited.

### Step 4: sorting and summary

After collecting $O(T^{n-1})$ candidates, sorting costs $O(T^{n-1} \log T)$. Overall:

- Time complexity: $O(T^{n})$, dominated by the enumeration (sorting is subsumed).
- Space complexity: $O(T^{n-1})$, mostly for storing candidates. The DFS recursion stack depth is only $O(n)$ and negligible.

One last point that is easy to misread: $T^n$ looks polynomial, but $T$ here is the “numeric value of the amount”, not the “number of input elements”. By the standards of algorithm theory (measuring by input bit length), the amount $T$ needs only $\log T$ bits to represent (polynomial in the input value), yet the runtime is $T^n$ (exponential in the input length). This is pseudo-polynomial, much like the knapsack problem’s $O(nW)$.

That said, because we fix the item count $n$ to a small constant and cap the amount $T$, solving is very fast in practice and the polynomial growth can be ignored.

## Relation to Other Integer Programming Problems

This problem belongs to the **bounded change-making** family: make up a target amount $T$ from several “coins” (here, items and plastic bags) that have restricted denominations and bounded quantities. It is related to subset-sum, knapsack, and the Frobenius (coin) problem, and is **weakly NP-hard**.

Mathematically it can also be written as a pure integer program (IP): all variables $q_i, q_*$ are non-negative integers, subject to linear equality and inequality constraints. Standard IP methods generally take two directions:

- **Branch-and-bound (BB)**: solve the LP relaxation that drops the integrality constraints. For a quantity $q_i$ whose relaxed value $q_i^*$ is non-integer, branch into $q_i \le \lfloor q_i^* \rfloor$ and $q_i \ge \lceil q_i^* \rceil$, then prune subproblems that cannot improve the current best bound.
- **Cutting-plane / Gomory**: at the LP relaxation optimum, add an inequality that cuts off the fractional solution while keeping all integer solutions, then re-solve. Repeat until every variable is integral.

This implementation deliberately avoids both methods and uses an exhaustive DFS instead, for three reasons:

1. **The item scale is tiny.** $n$ is usually 2, and the price sets and bag limit are fixed constants. Exhaustive search already runs in milliseconds with no performance bottleneck.
2. **The goal is to list many solutions, not a single optimum.** This application lists the top three balanced and diverse combinations and supports “give me another batch” that excludes already-shown solutions. Once branch-and-bound prunes a branch, it discards every sub-optimal solution under it, making full enumeration harder.
3. **Simplicity.** Branch-and-bound and cutting-plane require an LP solver (such as the simplex method) and a branch tree, adding significant dependencies and complexity.

It is worth noting that if the question were only “can $T$ be made (yes/no)” or “how many ways are there”, dynamic programming (DP) solves it in $O(nT)$ time — asymptotically better than this implementation’s $O(T^{n})$.

But our goal is not just to decide or count. We need to “list several concrete solutions, with balance scores, and be able to list other feasible ones on demand”. Backtracking all qualifying combinations from a DP table under multi-objective ordering is in fact more cumbersome. And at $n = 2$, both approaches run in milliseconds. So this implementation is not asymptotically optimal, but at this scale it strikes the best balance between correctness and simplicity.
